**Summary**

- [ ] Mugwort is an invasive perennial plant that is widespread across North America and most common in the eastern United States and Canada.
- [ ] It grows along roadsides, forest edges, waste areas and lawns, and spreads aggressively through a network of underground stems (rhizomes), forming large stands of only mugwort.
- [ ] These stands push out native plants and can delay or disrupt the natural regrowth of plant communities.
- [ ] Mugwort pollen is a common cause of hay fever.

**History**

- [ ] Mugwort is native to Europe and eastern Asia, where it has long been used as a medicinal herb.
- [ ] Its seed may first have been brought to North America as early as the 1500s by Jesuit missionaries in Canada.
- [ ] It was also spread across the continent as a contaminant in ships' ballast and in nursery stock.

**Seasonal differences**

- [ ] Mugwort shoots come up in spring, and it flowers from July to late September with many tiny green flowers clustered at the ends of the stems.
- [ ] The leaves are covered underneath with woolly, silver-white hairs, and the plant has an aromatic, slightly pungent smell.
- [ ] The stems become somewhat woody as they age.

**How to handle it**

- [ ] Mugwort is hard to control because of its dense root system.
- [ ] Pulling it does not work and can even help it spread, because small pieces of root left in the soil grow into new plants (pieces as small as 2 cm can regrow).
- [ ] It also survives mowing, even when mowed for two years in a row.

**Summary**

- [ ] Garlic mustard is an invasive herb and one of the worst invaders of forests in the Northeast and Midwest.
- [ ] It is one of very few non-native plants that can take over the shady forest floor, where it forms dense stands that block light, water and nutrients from native wildflowers.
- [ ] It also releases chemicals that slow the growth of other plants.
- [ ] Removing it has been shown to bring back a greater variety of other plants, including tree seedlings.

**History**

- [ ] Garlic mustard comes from Europe and parts of Asia and is believed to have been brought to North America for medicine and food.
- [ ] The earliest known report of it growing in the United States is from 1868 on Long Island, New York.
- [ ] It has since spread across the eastern United States and Canada and as far west as Washington, Utah and British Columbia.

**Seasonal differences**

- [ ] Garlic mustard takes two years to complete its life.
- [ ] In the first year, seeds sprout in February or early March and grow into a low rosette of dark green, kidney-shaped, scalloped leaves.
- [ ] In the second year it grows a flower stalk: small white four-petaled flowers appear in early April, four-sided seedpods form in May, and the plant dies by June.
- [ ] Crushed leaves smell like garlic, which helps identify it.

**How to handle it**

- [ ] Hand-pulling can stop garlic mustard from spreading if it is done before the seeds form.
- [ ] At least the upper half of the root must come out, which is easiest in spring when the soil is soft, and pulling needs to be repeated for up to five years because seeds can survive that long in the soil.
- [ ] Seeds are mainly spread by people and animals carrying them, so limiting foot traffic and soil disturbance in the forest helps prevent new infestations.

- [ ] Its hollow stems are usually green with yellow joints during the growing season and turn yellow when dry in winter, and old dead stems stay standing in the stands.
