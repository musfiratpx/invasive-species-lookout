## Summary
Kudzu, nicknamed "the vine that ate the South", is a climbing vine in the bean family whose stems can grow 30 to 100 feet a year. It crowds out, out-competes and physically crushes native plants: it climbs into trees, competes with them for light and water, can strangle them, and its weight can pull weakened trees down. Because it fixes nitrogen in its roots, it thrives in poor soils where native plants struggle, and it can form huge stands of a single plant.

## History
Kudzu is native to China, Japan and the Indian subcontinent. It was introduced to North America in 1876 at the Japanese pavilion of the Philadelphia Centennial Exposition, then planted to shade porches in the South. In the early 1900s the government paid farmers to plant it for erosion control and livestock feed, and during the Great Depression the Civilian Conservation Corps planted thousands of acres on hillsides. It is now found in 30 states, most commonly in the South.

## Seasonal differences
Its fragrant red, purple or magenta flowers, with a grape-like smell, appear in mid- to late summer, but only on vines climbing upward in full sun, and usually not before the plant's third year. In winter, stems trailing along open ground tend to die back, while climbing vines grow thick bark and survive.

## How to handle it
In New York City parks like Kissena, cutting, killing or removing plants without permission from NYC Parks is against park rules, so report it instead.
