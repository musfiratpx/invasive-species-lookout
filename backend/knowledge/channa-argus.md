## Summary
The northern snakehead is a long, narrow fish, up to 4 feet long, with a large mouth, sharp teeth and blotchy, snake-like markings on its head. It is a top predator: young snakeheads eat zooplankton, larvae and small fish, and adults eat fish, crustaceans, small amphibians and reptiles, and even some birds and mammals. It survives cold winters and low-oxygen water, can breathe air, and can move short distances over land. It is easily confused with the native bowfin and burbot.

## History
The northern snakehead is native to China and possibly Korea and Russia. It is believed to have reached the United States when aquarium owners dumped unwanted fish, or through intentional releases for food. In New York, the first one was documented in 2005 at Meadow Lake in Queens, and it is now considered established in New York. All snakeheads are listed as injurious wildlife under the federal Lacey Act.

## Seasonal differences
Snakeheads breed in summer, from June to August, building nests by weaving water plants into a column to protect their eggs. Parents guard their young for up to four weeks and can become very aggressive toward other animals during this time.

## How to handle it
NYIS advises that if you catch a snakehead, do not release it back into the water: kill it, freeze it in a double bag, and report the fish and its location to a natural resource agency. Note that NYC Parks rules say all fish caught in fresh water in city parks must be released immediately, so if you catch one in a city park, report it right away. In New York City parks like Kissena, park rules forbid chasing, harassing, harming or removing any animal, nest or eggs.
