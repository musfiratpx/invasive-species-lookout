## Summary
Mugwort is an invasive perennial plant that is widespread across North America and most common in the eastern United States and Canada. It grows along roadsides, forest edges, waste areas and lawns, and spreads aggressively through a network of underground stems (rhizomes), forming large stands of only mugwort. These stands push out native plants and can delay or disrupt the natural regrowth of plant communities. Mugwort pollen is a common cause of hay fever.

## History
Mugwort is native to Europe and eastern Asia, where it has long been used as a medicinal herb. Its seed may first have been brought to North America as early as the 1500s by Jesuit missionaries in Canada. It was also spread across the continent as a contaminant in ships' ballast and in nursery stock.

## Seasonal differences
Mugwort shoots come up in spring, and it flowers from July to late September with many tiny green flowers clustered at the ends of the stems. The leaves are covered underneath with woolly, silver-white hairs, and the plant has an aromatic, slightly pungent smell. The stems become somewhat woody as they age.

## How to handle it
Mugwort is hard to control because of its dense root system. Pulling it does not work and can even help it spread, because small pieces of root left in the soil grow into new plants (pieces as small as 2 cm can regrow). It also survives mowing, even when mowed for two years in a row. Its root pieces are easily spread when soil, yard waste and nursery plants are moved.
