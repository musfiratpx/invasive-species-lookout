## Summary
Water chestnut is a floating water plant that forms nearly impenetrable mats on lakes, ponds and slow rivers. The mats block light, killing native water plants beneath them, and when the plants rot each year they can lower oxygen in the water, harm aquatic life and even cause fish kills. The mats also get in the way of swimming, boating and fishing. It is not the water chestnut sold in cans at the supermarket.

## History
Water chestnut is native to parts of Europe, Asia and Africa. It was planted in a Harvard botanical garden around 1877 and escaped into the Charles River by 1879. It reached Collins Lake near Scotia, New York, around 1884, was established in the Hudson River by the early 1900s, and is now found across New York, from the Niagara Frontier and the Finger Lakes to Lake Champlain and Long Island.

## Seasonal differences
Water chestnut is an annual: it dies back each fall and regrows from seeds in spring. Each plant forms rosettes of floating triangular leaves with toothed edges. It starts producing fruit in July; each hard, nut-like fruit has four very sharp spines and ripens in about a month. The nuts sink and can stay alive in the mud for up to 12 years.

## How to handle it
Be careful near infested water: the sharp, spiny nuts can cause puncture injuries if stepped on, both to people walking along the shore and to animals. Because it is an annual, it can be controlled by stopping it from making seeds; small populations can be hand-pulled from canoes or kayaks.
