## Summary
Amur honeysuckle is a shrub, usually 6 to 15 feet tall, that forms very dense thickets that crowd out native plants and tree seedlings. It leafs out very early in spring, which gives it a head start over native plants. Its berries are less fatty and nutritious than native berries, which can harm migrating birds that eat lots of them. Invasive honeysuckles have hollow stems, while native honeysuckles have solid stems, which is one way to tell them apart.

## History
Amur honeysuckle is native to China, Japan and Korea. It was sent as seeds to arboretums across the United States in the late 1800s to see whether it would grow here, and was then planted for soil stabilization and wildlife until the mid-1980s. It escaped cultivation and is easily spread by birds that eat its berries.

## Seasonal differences
It is one of the first shrubs to leaf out in spring. From May to June it has small, very fragrant tube-shaped flowers, white to pink, and its dark-red berries don't ripen until late fall.

## How to handle it
Because birds spread the seeds, control should start in late summer or early fall, before the berries are ready. Seedlings and young plants can be removed by hand when there are only a few, repeated every year.
