## Summary
Cut-leaf teasel is a tall, very prickly plant that can grow up to about 3 meters (10 feet) tall. Large stands form a dense, tall layer above shorter plants, and can sharply reduce the number of plant species in an area. In New Jersey it has invaded limestone fens and caused rare plants, including the globally rare globe flower, to decline or disappear there. A large plant can make more than 2,000 seeds, and often 80% or more of them can sprout.

## History
Cut-leaf teasel is native to temperate Europe and Asia and has been known in the United States since the 1800s. It is sometimes sold for gardens and for dried flower arrangements. It is not as common as common teasel.

## Seasonal differences
It lives for one or more years as a low rosette of leaves, then flowers once and dies after setting seed. Its rosettes stay green in early spring and late fall, when many native plants are dormant.

## How to handle it
Mowing does not control it and often makes patches bigger, because mowing equipment spreads the seeds. Small rosettes can be dug up, but plants often resprout if any root is left. Flower stalks can be cut after flowering starts but before seeds form; the cut stalks must be taken away, because seeds can still ripen on them. Getting rid of it from a natural area can take five or six years of work.
