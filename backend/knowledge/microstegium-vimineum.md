## Summary
Japanese stiltgrass is a delicate, sprawling grass up to 3.5 feet tall that looks like a tiny bamboo. It spreads into dense stands in floodplains, forest edges, stream banks, fields, trails, ditches, lawns and gardens, crowding out native plants and lowering biodiversity. Deer and other grazers avoid it, which helps it win out over native plants. A quick way to recognize it: a stripe of silvery hairs runs down the middle of each leaf, slightly off-center.

## History
Japanese stiltgrass is native to India and Asia, including China, Japan and Korea. It was found in Tennessee around 1919, most likely brought in as packing material for porcelain. By 2011 it had been reported in 16 New York counties.

## Seasonal differences
It is an annual grass. In late summer and early fall, one or two thin flower spikes form at the top of each stem; each plant makes 100 to 1,000 seeds and then dies. Seeds can last at least three years in the soil and sprout readily after the ground is disturbed.

## How to handle it
It is shallow-rooted and easy to pull. Pull it in late summer, before seeds form, but not before July, or seeds already in the soil can still grow and go to seed that year. Its seeds spread in moved soil, in water and on the feet of animals and people.
