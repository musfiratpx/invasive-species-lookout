## Summary
Japanese honeysuckle is a woody vine that can reach 30 feet. Its vines cover native trees, and their weight can bring down weak trees; it also shades out the plants and tree seedlings below. Its leaves stay green into winter in mild weather, giving it a longer growing season than native plants, and it is the most shade-tolerant of the invasive honeysuckles. Invasive honeysuckles have hollow stems, while native honeysuckles have solid stems.

## History
Japanese honeysuckle is native to China, Japan and Korea. It was brought to Long Island, New York, in 1806 for gardens, and was widely sold as a garden plant until the early 1900s, when it was finally recognized as a weed. It escaped cultivation and is easily spread by birds.

## Seasonal differences
Its leaves are semi-evergreen and can stay green through mild winters. It flowers from May to June with small, very fragrant tube-shaped flowers, creamy white to pink, and produces dark-purple or black berries in the fall.

## How to handle it
Because birds spread the seeds, control should start in late summer or early fall, before the berries are ready. Seedlings and young plants can be removed by hand when there are only a few, repeated every year. In New York City parks like Kissena, cutting, killing or removing plants without permission from NYC Parks is against park rules, so report it instead.
