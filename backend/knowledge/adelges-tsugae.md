## Summary
The hemlock woolly adelgid is a tiny, aphid-like insect that is a serious threat to hemlock trees in eastern North America. It is easiest to spot by the white, woolly wax masses, about half the size of a cotton swab, at the base of hemlock needles. It feeds on the tree's stored food, killing needles and twigs; heavy infestations can kill hemlocks in as little as four years. Losing hemlocks harms the cool, shady streams and habitats that fish, salamanders and other wildlife depend on.

## History
The hemlock woolly adelgid is native to Japan and possibly China. It was first found on the east coast of North America in Richmond, Virginia, in the mid-1950s, likely brought accidentally from southern Japan, and has since spread to 18 eastern states. It was first detected in New York in the early 1980s, starting on Long Island and in the Hudson Valley.

## Seasonal differences
The white woolly masses appear in late winter and stay visible all year. Females lay eggs from March through May, and tiny reddish-brown crawlers hatch from April to June; crawlers are spread by wind, birds' feet and the fur of small mammals. A second generation lays eggs in June and July, and its young rest until late October before feeding again over the winter.

## How to handle it
In New York City parks like Kissena, park rules forbid chasing, harassing, harming or removing any animal, nest or eggs.
