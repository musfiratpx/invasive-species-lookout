## Summary
Japanese virgin's bower, also called sweet autumn clematis, is a garden vine that can climb 30 feet when it has support. It grows so fast and so heavily that in natural areas it can smother mature trees and push out native plants. It is on New York's regulated list: it may still be sold and planted, but not where it threatens natural areas, and it must be sold with signs saying it is invasive. It looks very much like the native virgin's bower, whose leaves are toothed and lobed instead of smooth-edged.

## History
Japanese virgin's bower is native to Japan and China and was brought to North America as an ornamental plant. It is still widely planted in gardens for its masses of small white flowers and feathery seedheads.

## Seasonal differences
New shoots grow from last year's woody stems in spring. It flowers in August, covering itself in a froth of small, four-petaled white flowers, which is when it is easiest to spot. Seeds form by late September, with silvery, feathery, curving seedheads that are spread by the wind.

## How to handle it
Plants and their roots can be dug out, but root pieces may resprout and seeds in the soil can keep sprouting for some time, so repeat visits may be needed. Planting the native virgin's bower instead gives a similar look without the invasive risk.

## Look-alikes
It looks very much like the native virgin's bower, whose leaves are toothed and lobed instead of smooth-edged.
