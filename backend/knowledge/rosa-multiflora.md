## Summary
Multiflora rose is a fast-growing shrub that can reach 10 to 15 feet tall and form dense, thorny thickets that crowd out native plants. It invades open woods, forest edges, fields, fence lines, roadsides and the edges of marshes. You can tell it apart from other roses by the pair of fringed bracts (stipules) at the base of each leaf stalk, and its stems usually have many curved thorns. A single large plant can produce thousands of seeds that stay alive in the soil for up to 20 years.

## History
Multiflora rose is native to eastern China, Japan and Korea. It was brought to the United States from Japan in 1866 as a rootstock for garden roses. In the 1930s the U.S. Soil Conservation Service promoted it for erosion control and as "living fences" to hold in livestock, and it was also planted for wildlife cover and later in highway medians. It then became a problem in pastures and fields and is now found in 41 states.

## Seasonal differences
Multiflora rose blooms in late May or June with clusters of fragrant white to pale-pink flowers. Small fruits (hips) form over the summer and turn red by mid-September. The hips stay on the plant through the winter.

## How to handle it
The stems usually have many curved thorns. Small seedlings can be pulled by hand, and smaller plants can be dug out, but all the roots must be removed because they can resprout. Cutting or mowing three to six times per growing season for two to four years can kill it. Because its seeds last for years in the soil, follow-up treatments are likely to be needed.

## Look-alikes
Native roses grow throughout New York City; the ones you are most likely to meet are Virginia rose and Carolina rose. Multiflora rose has a comb-like fringe on the leafy wings (stipules) at the base of each leaf, 5 to 11 leaflets, clusters of many small white flowers, and curved thorns with a wide base like a cat's claw. Native roses have stipules without a fringe, 5 to 7 leaflets, one or a few large flowers per cluster, and straight thorns.
