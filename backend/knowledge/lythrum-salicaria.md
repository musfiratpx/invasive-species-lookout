## Summary
Purple loosestrife is a tall wetland plant, up to about 13 feet, with showy spikes of purple flowers. It has invaded wetlands across North America, growing in marshes, along ponds, lakes, streams and ditches, and sometimes in drier meadows. Once established, its huge seed output and dense growth crowd out native plants, can keep native waterfowl and other birds from nesting, and can trap sediment and clog waterways.

## History
Purple loosestrife is native to Europe, Asia and northern Africa. It was probably brought to North America several times, both by accident in ships' ballast and on purpose as an herbal remedy for digestive problems. It was well established in New England by the 1830s and spread along canals and other waterways; it is now found throughout Canada and in every U.S. state except Hawaii and Florida.

## Seasonal differences
Shoots come up as early as late April, and flowering starts by mid-June. Seeds begin forming by late July and continue into autumn; one plant can make over 2 million seeds. The plant dies back at the first frost, but its dead stems stay standing through the winter.

## How to handle it
Small patches can be pulled by hand, but the whole root crown must come out. Mowing or otherwise damaging the plants can spread pieces that regrow. Removing it takes several years to clear out the adult plants and the seeds in the soil. Four kinds of beetles have been released in the U.S. to feed on purple loosestrife, with some success. In New York City parks like Kissena, cutting, killing or removing plants without permission from NYC Parks is against park rules, so report it instead.
