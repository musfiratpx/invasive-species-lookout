## Summary
The Asian shore crab is a small crab, about 1.4 to 1.7 inches across, with a square shell that has three spines on each side. Its shell can be green, red, orange-brown or purple; its claws have red spots and its legs have light and dark bands. It eats almost anything, including seaweed, salt marsh grass, young fish and small invertebrates, so it could harm native fish, shellfish and crabs and out-compete native mud crabs, blue crabs and lobsters.

## History
The Asian shore crab is native to the western Pacific Ocean, from Russia to Hong Kong and Japan. No one knows exactly how it reached the U.S. Atlantic coast, but it is thought that adults or larvae arrived in ballast water released by international ships. In New York it is known from the Hudson River and its lower tributaries and from Oyster Bay on Long Island's north shore.

## Seasonal differences
It breeds from May to September, and a female can produce three or four batches of eggs each season, each with up to 50,000 eggs. Its tiny larvae drift in the water for about a month before settling, so they can be carried long distances.

## How to handle it
In New York City parks like Kissena, park rules forbid chasing, harassing, harming or removing any animal, nest or eggs.
