## Summary
Mile-a-minute weed is a fast-growing, barbed vine that can grow up to six inches a day. It forms dense mats that climb over and smother other plants, shrubs and even trees, blocking their light and weighing them down until they weaken or die. It reduces native plants and the wildlife that depend on them, and its barbed thickets also discourage people from using an area. You can recognize it by its triangle-shaped leaves and the round, cup-like leafy collars around its stem.

## History
Mile-a-minute weed is native to India and eastern Asia. It was accidentally brought to York County, Pennsylvania, in 1930 in contaminated holly seed. In New York it has been recorded mostly in counties south of the northern Connecticut border, and it is estimated to occupy only about 20% of its potential U.S. range.

## Seasonal differences
Seeds sprout from early April through early July. It flowers and makes fruit over a long period, from June through October, producing spikes of blue, berry-like fruits. Frost kills the vines, and the seeds spend the winter in the soil, where they can stay alive for up to six years. Birds carry the seeds long distances, and the seeds can also float in water for a week or more.

## How to handle it
The vines and leaf undersides are covered in backward-pointing barbs. Hand-pulling works best early in the season, before the barbs harden; after that, thicker gloves are needed. Later in the season, pull carefully so the fruit isn't knocked off and spread. A tiny weevil that feeds only on mile-a-minute has been released in the U.S. and is currently the most promising control.
