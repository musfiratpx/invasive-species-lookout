## Summary
Pale swallow-wort, also called dog-strangling vine, is a climbing vine that forms large patches that grow over and smother native plants. Fewer insects and other arthropods live on it than on native plants, and in New York it is invading globally rare limestone habitats called alvars. Monarch caterpillars cannot survive on it, and it can crowd out the milkweed monarchs need. Its small, star-shaped flowers are pink to maroon.

## History
Pale swallow-wort is native to Ukraine and southeastern European Russia. It may first have been brought to North America as an ornamental plant, and it is most abundant in the lower Great Lakes region.

## Seasonal differences
Shoots come up in spring, the plants flower in early summer, and seed pods 1.5 to 3 inches long form in late July and early August. The seeds are carried by the wind, and a square meter of swallow-wort can produce 1,000 to 2,000 seeds a year.

## How to handle it
Its deep, fibrous roots make it hard to remove; if digging it up, get as much of the root crown as possible. For small patches, plants and seed pods can be bagged before they release seed. Mowing won't get rid of it, and mowing after seeds form can spread them. In New York City parks like Kissena, cutting, killing or removing plants without permission from NYC Parks is against park rules, so report it instead.
