## Summary
Lesser celandine, also called fig buttercup, is a low-growing spring plant that forms mats of smooth, kidney-shaped leaves and yellow flowers. It likes moist soil along streams and in floodplains, but as a garden escape it also grows in city and suburban areas. Because it comes up earlier than most native plants, it can crowd out native spring wildflowers such as spring beauties, trilliums and bloodroot, which are an early source of nectar for bees and other insects.

## History
Lesser celandine is native to Europe, Asia and northern Africa and was probably brought to North America as a garden plant. The oldest known herbarium specimen in the United States was collected in Pennsylvania in 1867. It is now reported throughout the northeastern United States, west to Missouri, and in the Pacific Northwest.

## Seasonal differences
Lesser celandine is a short-lived spring plant. Its shoots come up from late March to mid-April, and it flowers from late April to mid-May. By summer the leaves die back and the plant goes dormant underground, leaving bare ground that other weedy plants can move into.

## How to handle it
Lesser celandine is hard to control because it spreads from small underground tubers and from tiny bulb-like buds (bulbils) that form on the plant. Small patches can be dug out, but every tuber must be removed from the soil. Mowing early in the season can slow it but risks spreading the bulbils. Herbicides can also be effective with less soil disturbance; NYIS notes to always check state and local regulations and follow label instructions.
