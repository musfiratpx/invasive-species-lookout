## Summary
Japanese knotweed is a tall, shrubby plant, 3 to 15 feet tall, with hollow, bamboo-like stems, which is why it is sometimes called Japanese bamboo. It spreads quickly into thick stands that shade out native plants, leaving bare ground underneath that erodes easily, which is especially harmful along streams. It tolerates deep shade, heat, salty soil and drought, and once established it is extremely hard to get rid of.

## History
Japanese knotweed comes from eastern Asia (Japan, China and Korea). It was brought to U.S. estates as an ornamental plant in the late 1800s and was also used for erosion control. By the mid-1890s it had been reported near Philadelphia, in Schenectady, New York, and in New Jersey, and by the late 1930s it was already seen as a pest.

## Seasonal differences
Shoots come up each spring from a network of underground stems that can stretch 65 feet or more. Branched sprays of small greenish-white flowers bloom from August to September, followed by small winged fruits. The stems are smooth and hollow, with a swollen joint where each leaf attaches.

## How to handle it
Young plants can sometimes be pulled by hand, but any root left behind can resprout. All removed plant parts should be bagged and disposed of securely. It should not be mowed or cut with string trimmers, because the pieces get moved around and resprout, spreading it instead of controlling it.
